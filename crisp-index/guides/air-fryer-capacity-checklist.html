Checklist · Choosing the shape

A no-hype checklist for comparing air fryer capacity

By The Crisp Index Editorial DeskNovember 20, 20266 min readCluster: Choosing the shape

Capacity is the most quoted number on any air fryer listing — and the least understood. “8 quarts” is a volume. It is not a serving count, not a batch guarantee, and not a promise about how much food will actually crisp. Here’s how to compare capacity without falling for the number.

What a capacity figure does tell you

It’s a rough sense of how much air the chamber holds. Two machines with the same figure can cook very differently depending on shape (deep basket vs wide rack), how the heat moves, and how much clearance the manufacturer demands around the food.

What it doesn’t

  • It isn’t servings. Family-size claims on listings are marketing language; the number behind them isn’t standardized.
  • It isn’t usable area. Air needs to move around food. A crowded basket steams; it doesn’t crisp. Whatever the volume says, the food has to fit in one layer (or a thin one) to cook the way you’re imagining.
  • It isn’t shared. On dual-basket machines, the headline figure is often the sum of two smaller baskets — each of which cooks independently.
  • It isn’t counter space. Big capacity has a footprint, a height (check cabinet clearance), and sometimes a door swing.

The checklist

  • Measure your counter — width, depth, and the vertical gap to any cabinet above. Tape it out before you shop.
  • List your top five meals for this machine. Write down the largest single item (a whole small chicken? a 12-inch frozen pizza? two layers of fries?).
  • Match shape to meal: tumbling food → basket; flat and layered → racks.
  • Decide single vs dual zone — two baskets let you finish two foods together (look for a finish-together feature on the listing), but each zone is smaller.
  • Ask what fits in one layer at the advertised capacity. If the listing can’t answer, assume the real number is smaller.
  • Check the accessories in the box — racks and trays change what fits; splitters can double layers or halve your usable space.
  • Plan for the gap factor: budget for less than the headline capacity every time. If dinner is one item for two people, a smaller machine with usable space beats a big one that’s always crowded.

How this shows up on real listings

On our test queue, capacity language ranges from “6 Quart” with two “3Qt” baskets, to “8 Qt XL”, to a “26QT” French-door oven — three different numbers answering three different questions. That’s exactly why the shape question comes first. Listing wording is quoted verbatim in the register with its source link; none of it is independently tested yet, and we say so.

Skip the purchase if…

  • …you can’t name the largest thing you’d realistically cook in it.
  • …it only fits your counter with the door or baskets permanently open.
  • …the listing answers capacity with adjectives (“family size”, “XL”) instead of measurements.
