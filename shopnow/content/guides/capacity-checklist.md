## Why the number in the title is the least useful number

"6 QT" tells you the volume of something. It does not tell you the volume of the space your food sits in, whether that space is one basket or two, or whether the manufacturer measured to the rim or to the fill line. Two appliances with the same advertised quartage can hold visibly different amounts of a single layer of wings, because capacity in a convection cavity is mostly about *surface you can lay food out on*.

Air fryers cook by moving hot air across the food. Crowding the basket defeats the mechanism - the food steams instead of browning. So the usable capacity of a large, crowded basket can be smaller than the usable capacity of a smaller basket you can actually spread out in.

Here is what to check instead.

## The eight checks

**1. Single layer, not total volume.**
Estimate the floor area of the basket in inches. That is your real capacity line. A round basket 8 inches across holds roughly one layer of about a dozen wings. Multiply that area by however many baskets or racks there are - and stop calling quarts the answer.

**2. Depth matters more than diameter for anything with a curve.**
Thighs, small whole fish, a stuffed pepper, a rosemary potato: shallow baskets force these into a single messy layer and tall narrow ones let them sit. If you cook mostly flat things, ignore depth. If you cook mostly round things, it is the number to look for.

**3. Fill line versus rim.**
Some coatings and instructions cap the fill below the rim. A basket measured to the rim overstates what you may load. If the listing shows a maximum-fill marking in a photo, that photo is worth more than the spec table.

**4. Two baskets is not one big basket.**
A dual-basket model splits the cavity. Two 3-quart baskets do not make a 6-quart basket; they make two 3-quart baskets, which is better for two foods finishing at once and worse for one big item. Decide which of those you need before you let the total quart number impress you.

**5. How many people, honestly.**
The workable rule of thumb to check against your own habit: a basket sized for one to two people gets used several times a week; a basket sized for six sits in a cupboard by month three. Oversized is not "safe room to grow". Oversized is friction - more to wash, more to store, longer to heat.

**6. What goes in the dishwasher is what gets cleaned.**
Capacity that requires hand-washing a scratched coated basket gets downgraded by use. People cook smaller portions in a machine that could cook bigger ones. Check the cleaning instructions at the same time as the capacity, in that order if you have to pick a priority.

**7. Reheat capacity is the hidden use case.**
Pizza, fries, roasted vegetables, chicken: a reheating surface that crisps beats an oven for four out of five weekday jobs. If you mostly want a crisping surface, the right "capacity" is the size of a plate, and a mid basket can beat a large oven-style machine for that use.

**8. Leftovers need the biggest number.**
The one place capacity genuinely runs out is cooking once and eating three times. If that is your pattern, a big single cavity plus a rack beats two small baskets, because a tray of sausage or a whole bird does not split.

## The three sentences that predict regret

From the pattern of what people complain about in this category, the regret sentences are almost always:

- "It is bigger than I thought and it lives in the hall cupboard now."
- "I wish it held a whole dinner, so I cook two batches every time."
- "The coating is going and it is not dishwasher-safe."

Notice that two of the three are about size relative to *your* kitchen, and none is about the brand. Write your own version of the first sentence before you buy. If you can't, you have not decided yet - you have just picked a colour.
