<p class="lede">Capacity is the spec air fryer marketing squeezes hardest. "Family size," "XL," extra quarts counted a certain way. The good news: capacity is also the easiest claim to test against your own life, because you already know what you cook and how many people show up.</p>

<h2>What does the quart number actually measure?</h2>

<p>Quarts (or liters) describe interior volume. But air frying works by moving hot air <em>around</em> food in a single layer. A tall, narrow cavity can quote more volume than a wide, shallow one while fitting fewer fries at once. Volume is a fact; usable cook area is the number that changes dinner.</p>

<p class="note">Rule of thumb: when comparing two models, ask "how much food lies flat at the same time?" before "how many quarts is it?"</p>

<h2>What capacity do you actually need?</h2>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Cooking for one or two:</strong> a 4–6 quart basket handles most meals in one batch, or two quick batches.</li>
  <li><strong>Three to four people:</strong> 6–10 quarts, or a dual-basket unit where each side runs its own batch in parallel, is where most households land.</li>
  <li><strong>Five or more, or you meal-prep:</strong> oven-style with real rack area, or multiple batches with a bigger basket. Batch cooking is normal for every air fryer — plan for it instead of paying to avoid it.</li>
</ul>

<p>These are planning ranges, not rankings. The register's own units — <a href="../../shop/">6-quart dual-basket</a>, <a href="../../shop/">8-quart single-basket</a>, and a <a href="../../shop/">26-quart oven-style</a> — span exactly this range, which is why we carry all three rather than declaring one size "best."</p>

<h2>The checklist</h2>

<ol>
  <li><strong>Count your real portions first.</strong> Write down the three things you cook most and how many servings each makes. That list is your true capacity spec.</li>
  <li><strong>Look for interior dimensions or food-on-tray photos</strong> on the official listing, not just the headline quart number. A standard dinner plate (~10–11 inches) is a useful ruler.</li>
  <li><strong>Separate total volume from basket count.</strong> "Two 3-quart baskets" (as one register product lists) cooks two <em>different</em> foods at once; a single 8-quart basket cooks one big batch. Different tools, different weeknights.</li>
  <li><strong>Check the max weight or food guidance in the manual</strong> if the listing states one. Capacity claims without any food context are the ones to discount.</li>
  <li><strong>Beware the "fits a whole chicken" claim without dimensions.</strong> Whole chickens vary more than the photos suggest. Ask for the roasting rack size instead.</li>
  <li><strong>Compare batch time, not just batch size.</strong> If a smaller unit does two 12-minute batches and a bigger one does one 20-minute batch, the difference is smaller than the quart gap implies.</li>
  <li><strong>Read the return window before Black Friday.</strong> The only honest capacity test is a real cook in your kitchen. Buy where returns are easy.</li>
</ol>

<h2>How to use this against a product page</h2>

<p>Open the official listing (every product in <a href="../../compare/">our comparison table</a> links to one) and fill a scrap of paper with just four facts: cook area or interior dimensions, basket count, max temperature, and whether the food parts are dishwasher-safe. If a listing won't tell you those four things, that silence is itself a finding.</p>

<h2>The quiet part</h2>

<p>Almost every household that buys "enough capacity" still cooks in batches on holidays. Paying a large premium to avoid batches you'll occasionally run anyway is the most common capacity mistake. Match the machine to your Tuesday, and handle Thanksgiving with two rounds.</p>
