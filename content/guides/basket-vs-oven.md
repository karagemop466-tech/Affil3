<p class="lede">Everyone asks "which air fryer is best?" The more useful question is quieter: <strong>what are you actually going to cook in it?</strong> Basket-style and oven-style air fryers answer that question differently, and the gap between them shows up long before price.</p>

<h2>What does each shape actually do well?</h2>

<p>A <strong>basket air fryer</strong> is a heating element, a fan, and a perforated basket. Food sits close to the heat and gets moved by the airflow. It excels at things you'd otherwise deep-fry or re-crisp: fries, wings, vegetables, frozen snacks, leftovers. You shake the basket mid-cook; that's the whole ritual.</p>

<p>An <strong>oven-style air fryer</strong> (sometimes called an air fryer oven or toaster oven with an air-fry mode) is a small convection oven. Racks replace the basket, so you get layers, a wider footprint for food like pizza or salmon fillets, and usually extra modes — bake, broil, toast, dehydrate, rotisserie depending on the model.</p>

<p>Neither shape is "more powerful" as a category. What changes is the <em>kind</em> of cooking each one makes convenient.</p>

<h2>Which questions separate the two?</h2>

<ul>
  <li><strong>What's the default weeknight meal?</strong> If it's a pile of something crispy for one to three people, a basket is the short path. If it's a protein plus sides, or food that needs a tray, the oven shape earns its footprint.</li>
  <li><strong>How many people are you feeding at once?</strong> Baskets are fastest for small batches. Oven-style units hold more flat area but rarely cook "twice as fast" — compare cook <em>area</em>, not marketing capacity numbers.</li>
  <li><strong>Do you reheat food you already cooked?</strong> Both do it well, but oven-style handles slices, bakes and open-faced food without a basket's shape limit.</li>
  <li><strong>How much counter are you giving up permanently?</strong> Basket units are usually cube-shaped and compact; air fryer ovens are wide and tall. Measure the spot before you shop.</li>
  <li><strong>Who cleans it?</strong> Baskets and their trays are typically dishwasher-safe. Oven-style units have more racks, trays and a larger interior cavity — more parts, more surface.</li>
</ul>

<h2>What should you compare on the spec sheet?</h2>

<p>Whatever shape you lean toward, the numbers worth comparing are the same:</p>

<ol>
  <li><strong>Interior dimensions or cook area</strong>, in addition to the advertised quarts. Quarts alone don't tell you whether your food fits flat.</li>
  <li><strong>Temperature range and top-end mode.</strong> A higher "crisp" or "fry" setting (some models reach 450°F) is a finishing tool, not a health claim.</li>
  <li><strong>Preheat expectations.</strong> Basket units typically need none; ovens may want a couple of minutes. It changes weeknight math.</li>
  <li><strong>Parts that touch food.</strong> Nonstick coatings, dishwasher safety, and how many separate pieces you'll actually wash.</li>
  <li><strong>Noise and venting.</strong> Every air fryer moves a lot of air. Where the exhaust points matters in a small kitchen.</li>
</ol>

<h2>So which one?</h2>

<p>If you can answer "crispy snacks and sides, fast, for a few people" without hesitating, start with a basket. If your honest answer involves trays, racks, baking, or feeding four to six at once, the oven shape is doing work a basket can't. The rest — brand, presets, colors — follows that decision rather than leading it.</p>
