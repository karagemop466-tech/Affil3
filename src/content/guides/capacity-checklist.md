Capacity is the number every brand leads with and the one that means least, because it is a volume and cooking is a surface area. Here is the whole trick: stop comparing quarts and start comparing how much food can sit in a single layer, exposed to moving hot air.

## What the four common claims are actually saying

- **"5.5 qt / 5.2 L"** — cavity volume. Includes the space the basket walls, the fan housing and the air gap eat. Compare it only between units of the same shape.
- **"Serves 4–6"** — a portion claim, usually for a single food, chosen by the marketer. There is no standard behind it and no reason to trust it.
- **"A 5 lb chicken fits"** — the most useful sentence on many listings, because a bird tells you the internal height and width at once. Only useful if you cook chickens.
- **Accessory photos** — a shallow wide basket, a deep drum, a tray with edges: the pictures show you the cooking surface better than the spec table does.

## The checklist

Print it, or just keep it open in a second tab while you read a listing. Each line has a place to look, and none of them need a lab.

1. **Single-layer portions.** How many things can lie flat without touching? Find it in internal dimensions, or in the accessory image, or in the "serves" claim read sceptically.
2. **Cooking area, not volume.** Multiply length × width of the basket or tray when the listing publishes it. Two units, both 6 qt, can differ by 40% in usable area.
3. **Batch count for your actual dinner.** Write down your most common meal and how many batches it takes. Multiply by the minutes per batch. That is the number that decides whether you use this in March.
4. **What you cannot cook.** A tall narrow basket holds no sheet pan, no 12 muffins, no whole tray of anything. If you want crisp leftovers in quantity, width beats depth.
5. **Shake-and-flip freedom.** Basket units let you shake mid-cook, which matters for fries and roasted vegetables and is nearly irrelevant for a steak. Oven units with racks let you cook two levels at once, which is how they honestly earn the extra quarts.
6. **Rack and tray extras.** Units that ship with a second rack effectively double usable area without using more counter. Units that sell it separately cost more than the price difference you were comparing.
7. **Clearance numbers, not the marketing dimensions.** Vent clearance is often listed in the manual and never in the bullet points. Search the listing for "clearance" or "space" before you decide the footprint fits.

:::note A cheap physical test
If you can get near the thing in a shop, put your two hands inside the basket, flat, side by side. That is roughly the useful single-layer area of a small unit, and it is a faster, truer measurement than any table on the internet.
:::

## When a bigger capacity is a worse buy

Bigger costs you counter space, preheat time, washing-up and — the part nobody mentions — a worse result on small meals. A 9-quart oven-style unit cooking one burger is a convection oven with extra steps: the fan moves air around a big empty cavity and the bun dries before the meat colours. If you mostly cook for one or two, the honest recommendation is the smallest footprint that fits your one batch of the thing you eat most.

## What this checklist cannot tell you

Whether the coating survives a year of dishwashing, whether the display is readable at an angle, whether the drawer sticks when it is hot, whether the fan is a 60-decibel hum or a 75-decibel whine you will hear from the next room. Those are longevity and experience questions, and only long ownership answers them.
