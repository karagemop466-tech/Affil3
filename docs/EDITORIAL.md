# Editorial standard

## Voice

Second person. Short declarative sentences. Concrete nouns. Numbers only when sourced. The test for a
paragraph is whether it changes what the reader does next.

Never: "in today's fast-paced world", "elevate your cooking", "game-changer", "top-notch", "we've got you
covered", a colon-headed list of adjectives, or a summary of the article at the end of the article. No
"Whether you're a seasoned chef or a beginner". No sentence that could be pasted into an article about a
blender. Read one paragraph aloud: if it could describe any product on earth, delete it.

Rhythm that works here: a specific observation, then the thing to do about it, then what remains unknown.
Example of the register we want — *"A basket and an oven-style unit can quote the same number of quarts
and hold completely different amounts of dinner, because the number describes the cavity, not the cooking
surface."* It earns its space by changing a decision.

## The five standing rules, and why each is non-negotiable

1. **No product claim without a source row.** A claim about a product that we cannot stand behind is the
   specific thing the Participation Requirements prohibit ("inaccurate, overbroad, deceptive or otherwise
   misleading"). If the source does not exist, the sentence does not.
2. **No invented authority.** No lab, no hours-of-testing count, no "our team of experts", no rating. The
   byline is a collective because no named author has been approved (Q-10). Fabricating a reviewer is worse
   than an anonymous method note, and it is a lie the reader can check.
3. **Say what is missing before saying what you think.** Every guide carries the standing "what we did not
   test" block, generated by the build so it cannot be dropped by an editor in a hurry.
4. **Question-shaped, not verdict-shaped, until there is evidence.** Titles are the buyer's own question.
   The brief's working titles are treated as hypotheses, not as claims about demand or product superiority.
5. **Cadence caps at what one person can verify.** Two pieces a week, not two a day. Volume without
   evidence is the thin-affiliate pattern Google's spam policies name, and it is why most sites like this
   one die.

## Per-piece checklist

- [ ] Claims table filled: one row per factual sentence, each naming a `data/sources.json` id and a date.
- [ ] No price, rating, review-quote or Amazon-image content.
- [ ] Disclosure on the page (the layout does this — never remove it to win a few pixels of fold).
- [ ] Any time-limited or seasonal sentence has a `review_by` date, or is not written at all.
- [ ] Reader path: does the piece say what to check, where to check it, and what we have not checked?
- [ ] `python3 tools/build_site.py && python3 tools/check_site.py` passes.
- [ ] Calendar row moved to `published`, in the same commit as the file.
- [ ] Read once on a phone, where most of it will be read.

## What "AI-assisted" means here, precisely

* **Permitted**: scaffolding a page; checking that the registers agree; generating the sitemap and feed;
  validating link format; catching expired seasonal copy; drafting from *our own* recorded observations;
  proposing headline variants for a human to choose between.
* **Not permitted, per the licence**: any use of Program Content to build or improve a model; scraping or
  warehousing listing, price or review data; API use for aggregation or analysis without prior written
  approval; automated interaction with Amazon that does not identify itself as an Agent (IP License §4).
* **Not permitted, per the FTC and per honesty**: generated testimonials, invented testing, undisclosed
  compensation, or reviewing a product nobody touched.

The distinction is not squeamishness. The first group makes a small team faster. The second group is how a
site gets its account closed, which ends the first group permanently.
