The case for a probe

Knowing when food is done: why a probe beats a guess

Every appliance on this desk asks you to trust a timer. A thermometer is the only tool here that answers the question the timer is guessing at.

SN By ShopNow Labs editorial desk · Published 2026-11-20 · 6 min read
I get commissions for purchases made through links in this post.

The timer is not measuring your food

A recipe says twenty minutes. The appliance counts down twenty minutes. Nothing in that loop has measured the food. It measured the clock.

That is fine when you are reheating something already cooked. It is unreliable when you are cooking a protein to a target, because the variables that change the time — starting temperature, thickness, how full the basket is, how many times you opened the door — are all invisible to a timer.

An internal-temperature probe replaces "twenty minutes" with "four degrees to go".

What the CHEF iQ Sense actually claims

The listing describes it as a smart wireless meat thermometer with three ultra-thin probes, five sensors, a 1000°F heat rating, WiFi, and use across BBQ, grill, oven, smoker and air fryer. The generation named in the title is Gen3.

Those are the manufacturer's claims as stated on the listing. Three probes means you can track three pieces of food, or three parts of one large piece, at once. The 1000°F rating matters mainly if you cook over high heat or in a very hot oven; the probes' stated tolerance is well above the temperatures a home air fryer reaches.

Why this product is on an air fryer desk

Buying an air fryer and buying a probe look like unrelated purchases. They are usually the same purchase: someone who is not confident about doneness buys the appliance, then still is not confident about doneness. The probe is the cheaper half of that solution and it works across every cooker you already own.

What to check before buying any wireless probe

How does the reading reach you? If it goes to a phone app, the app has to work, the phone has to be nearby, and the connection has to hold. WiFi at range is a different claim from Bluetooth at range. Check which radios the model uses and whether a display or base unit is included, because an app-only probe is only as reliable as the app.

How many probes, and do they need to be charged? Three probes is generous for home cooking. What matters more is whether each one needs its own charging and whether you can use one while another charges.

What is the probe diameter? "Ultra-thin" is a claim about how much of a hole it leaves in the meat. Thin probes are easier to insert into a chicken breast without a puncture channel leaking juices. The listing says ultra-thin but does not state a diameter in the text retrieved, so treat the exact figure as unconfirmed.

What is it rated to, and what happens above that? The listing states 1000°F. Probes have a limit; exceeding it once can end the probe. If you cook over a ripping-hot grill, check the rating rather than assuming.

How do you clean it? Probes are hand-wash items with electronics in the handle end. Neither the probe nor the cable should go in a dishwasher. This is the one appliance on this desk with essentially no cleanup burden, but it also has no dishwasher-safe answer.

Does it need a subscription? Some smart cooking devices tie features to an app account. Check whether the temperature monitoring works without an account, before you buy into an ecosystem.

How to actually use one

The useful habit is small: pick a target internal temperature for the food, insert the probe in the thickest part, and stop cooking when it is reached rather than when the timer says so.

  • Start with the food's target temperature from a trusted cooking source for that protein and cut.
  • Insert into the thickest part, avoiding bone and avoiding the very centre of a very thin piece.
  • Leave it in through any resting period if you want to see the carry-over rise.
  • Use one probe per piece of food rather than moving one probe between pieces, which is what a three-probe design is for.

Minimum internal temperatures, from the USDA

These are the US Department of Agriculture's Food Safety and Inspection Service figures, taken from its Safe Minimum Internal Temperature Chart. They are a food-safety standard, not a preference, and they are higher priority than any recipe time.

| Food | Minimum internal temperature | Rest | |---|---|---| | Beef, pork, veal and lamb steaks, chops and roasts | 145°F (62.8°C) | 3 minutes | | Ground meats | 160°F (71.1°C) | none stated | | All poultry, including ground poultry | 165°F (73.9°C) | none stated | | Fish and shellfish | 145°F (62.8°C) | none stated | | Leftovers and casseroles | 165°F (73.9°C) | none stated | | Eggs | 160°F (71.1°C) | none stated |

Source: USDA FSIS, Safe Minimum Internal Temperature Chart, read 2026-11-20. The chart also notes that consumers may choose to cook to higher temperatures for personal preference. For anything not listed, check the chart directly rather than relying on this table.

Where a probe does not help

A probe tells you the temperature of the food, not the state of the appliance. If an air fryer's heating element is failing — a pattern reported by some customers in the review summaries of two listings on this desk — the probe will show you the food stalling, which is useful diagnosis, but the appliance is still the problem.

A probe also does not tell you whether something is crispy. Crispness is moisture and surface heat, and no internal thermometer measures it. For that, you still need to look at the food.
