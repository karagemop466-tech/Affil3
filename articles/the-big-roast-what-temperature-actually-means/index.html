The big roast: what temperature actually means

For the roast that only happens a few times a year, the thermometer is the one tool that removes the guesswork.

By ShopNow Kitchen editorial team · Published November 20, 2026 · Last reviewed November 20, 2026

The big roast is the one cook of the year where timing feels like a gamble. The cut is expensive, the cook is long, and the moment you are waiting for — is it done — arrives only once. The way to take the gamble out of it is to stop asking the clock and start asking the meat.

The targets, from the USDA

The USDA Food Safety and Inspection Service publishes the Safe Minimum Internal Temperature Chart. For the classic roast-table proteins, these are the figures it lists.

What you are roastingTarget
Beef, pork, veal, lamb (steaks, chops, roasts)145 degrees F, plus a 3-minute rest
Ground meats160 degrees F
All poultry, including ground poultry165 degrees F
Uncooked ham145 degrees F, plus a 3-minute rest

The rest time matters. For beef, pork, veal and lamb at 145 degrees F, the chart specifies a three-minute rest, during which the temperature continues to rise or equalise. A roast pulled at the exact moment it hits target, with no rest, is not the same roast.

Where to check it

The USDA's food-thermometer guidance is specific about placement, and it is the difference between a real reading and a lucky one. Insert the probe into the thickest part of the meat, away from bone, fat or the pan. For whole poultry, check the thickest part of the thigh, the wing and the breast.

  • Thickest part, not the edge or the thin end.
  • Avoid bone and fat, which read heat differently than muscle.
  • For a whole bird, sample several spots — thigh, wing, breast — because one spot is not the whole bird.

Why this is a thermometer job

A roast's doneness window is narrow and the cook is long. An instant-read probe gives you the number at the moment you need it; a wireless leave-in probe watches the cook and tells you when the target is reached. Both are built for exactly this moment, and both are on the thermometers we track.

Every temperature and placement rule above is reproduced from the USDA Food Safety and Inspection Service Safe Minimum Internal Temperature Chart and its food-thermometer guidance. We add no figures of our own.
