How to read air fryer specifications without being misled

Quarts, watts, degrees, presets: four numbers that get twisted into marketing, and what the maker actually published.

By ShopNow Kitchen editorial team · Published November 20, 2026 · Last reviewed November 20, 2026

An air fryer specification sheet is four numbers wearing a trench coat. Quarts, watts, degrees, presets. Each one is real, each one is published by the manufacturer, and each one turns into something else the moment it appears in a listing title. This guide is a short decoder: what each number is, what it is not, and where to go when the listing and the manufacturer disagree.

Quarts: capacity is a shape, not a number

A quart is a volume, and a chamber's volume does not tell you how much food you can cook in one go. It tells you the size of the space. The number that matters is how that space divides.

Our two-basket air fryer publishes 6 qt, and the same page breaks it down as two independent 3 qt baskets. Six quarts in one drawer would be one big dinner. Six quarts split across two drawers is two three-quart dinners, each with its own timer. The number on the box is the sum; the number on your counter is the part you use.

The same caution applies to our countertop oven, which publishes 26 qt / 24.6 L and states it is for 4-6 people, with two levels and a french door. More volume there means more levels, not one bigger batch. Compare the shape of the space, not just its size.

Capacity is not even a shared unit across this category. Our range spans two air fryers, one countertop oven, a handheld instant-read probe and a wireless leave-in probe. A quart figure on the first three has no meaning at all for the last two, which is why our comparison table leaves those cells blank instead of printing a dash.

Watts: a current question before it is a speed question

Most people read wattage as how fast the thing cooks. The more useful reading is how much current it pulls, because that determines whether you can run it at the same time as anything else.

1700 W at 120 V is about 14 amps. A standard kitchen circuit in the United States is 15 amps. So an appliance at 1700 W can use nearly all of it, and the toaster, the coffee maker and the kettle are all reaching for the same budget. It is not a fault in the appliance. It is a thing to know before you plug it in.

Our 8 qt air fryer makes the point in one line. The manufacturer's page states 1700 W. The Amazon listing for the same model number states 1000 W. We publish 1700 W, because that is the figure from the maker's own page, and we flag the listing rather than quietly choosing one. When a maker and a listing disagree, the maker's page is the primary source and the listing is evidence that the number circulates in two forms.

Degrees: a ceiling, a burst, and a range

A published temperature range is the appliance's operating envelope. Our two-basket air fryer publishes 200-450 °F, with the 450 °F attached to a named Hi-Fry setting. Our single-basket air fryer publishes 200-400 °F, with no separate burst described.

Then there is the number in the listing title. The 8 qt listing advertises a 450 °F Hi-Fry Option, while the manufacturer's page for that model states a maximum of 400 °F, and the listing's own bullet list also states 200-400 °F. A title is marketing copy that a manufacturer approved once; a specification page is the current published statement. When they conflict, we publish the specification page and flag the title.

Our countertop oven publishes no temperature range at all on the manufacturer's page, so its cell on our table is blank. That is a different kind of blank from a wrong number, and it is a legitimate thing to ask a retailer about before you buy.

Presets: a convenience, rarely a difference

Our single-basket air fryer publishes four cooking presets. Our two-basket unit publishes none on its specification page, and its listing does not state a count either. A preset is a saved setting for one food at one temperature. Whether four presets or none is the difference between a shortcut and nothing is a matter of whether you cook the same four things every week.

The oven is the interesting case. The manufacturer page shows 25 cooking pre-sets in its own specification block, while the Amazon listing title says 24 one-touch preset options, and the same page's description calls it a 10-in-1 appliance. The manufacturer's own pages disagree with each other on that count, so we publish no preset count for it at all rather than pick the number that reads best.

A five-step check you can run in two minutes

  1. Find the model number on the appliance or its listing, then open the manufacturer's page for that exact model. Model families share pages, and a nearby model's numbers are not this model's numbers.
  2. Read the specification block, not the feature bullets. The block is the list the maker stands behind; the bullets are written to be read on a phone while shopping.
  3. Write down each number next to the word that makes it honest: 6 qt, but two 3 qt baskets. 1700 W, but about 14 amps on a 15-amp circuit. 200-400 °F, but the title says 450.
  4. Leave a blank cell blank. If the maker does not publish it, the honest value is 'not stated', and that is more useful to you than a number copied from a title.
  5. Check the warranty and the certification while you are there. They are short lines and they are the two figures most often omitted from a listing title.

Questions readers ask next

Does a higher wattage mean it cooks faster?

It means it draws more power, and in these appliances more power generally means a hotter chamber faster. But the ceiling on how fast a piece of food cooks is usually its own thickness and moisture, not the appliance's wattage. We publish watts because they are published, not because we have measured a cooking difference. We are not a test kitchen and we do not claim to be.

Is a bigger chamber always better?

No, and the shape usually matters more than the volume. A chamber you can only fill half way wastes both the heat and the counter space. A chamber split into two smaller drawers is only an advantage when you are genuinely cooking two things at once.

Why does your table leave so many cells blank?

Because a blank cell on this site means 'not confirmed from an official page', and an unfilled cell is the honest answer when the maker does not publish a figure. Every blank has a line of explanation on the product page telling you exactly which figure is missing and why, and each one is listed on our Flagged for review page with the source that was checked.

Can I trust a listing title's numbers?

Treat a title as a claim someone made, not a specification. It is a perfectly good place to start a question. It is a bad place to end one. This site publishes figures from the manufacturer's specification pages and flags title claims that conflict with them.
