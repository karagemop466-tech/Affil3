How accurate does a meat thermometer need to be, and what does 'accurate' even mean?

A tolerance is not a measurement, and a number on a box is not a promise. Here is how to read an accuracy claim properly.

Autumn 2026 6 min read Published 2026-11-20 Desk: The Counter & Crumb desk
The short answer

You need far less accuracy than the packaging implies — a few degrees is enough for almost every cooking decision — but you need to understand what the number means before you compare two devices. A stated tolerance of ±0.5°F is a manufacturer claim about an instrument under specified test conditions. It is not a measurement you made, and it does not describe the error you will see in your kitchen.

The question

What accuracy do I actually need in a meat thermometer, and what does the stated figure mean?

A tolerance is a bound, not a reading

When a manufacturer writes ±0.5°F, they are saying that under their test conditions the instrument stayed within half a degree of a reference. That is a useful and meaningful claim. It is also, in laboratory terms, a bound on error.

Two things follow from it. First, the instrument is not always exactly right — it is within a window. Second, and more importantly, the window applies to the instrument reading a controlled reference at a controlled temperature. Your chicken breast in a windy garden is not a controlled reference at a controlled temperature.

The gap between an instrument's tolerance and its real-world reading is where probe placement error, ambient wind, evaporative cooling and bone contact all live. Those are bigger than a half degree, and no amount of precision in the instrument removes them.

How much accuracy is enough

For the decisions most people actually make, a couple of degrees changes nothing. Whether a chicken breast has reached 165°F or 160°F is rarely the difference between a good dinner and a bad one, because a chicken breast continues cooking off the heat after it leaves the heat.

Where accuracy does matter is thin cuts and rare beef, where the gap between rare and medium rare is a matter of a few degrees. That is exactly where the whole quality of your cook rests, and exactly where the probe placement matters most.

So the honest summary is: buy the better-reading device if you are chasing a precise medium rare, and do not agonise if you are roasting a chicken.

What to check instead of the number

The stated accuracy figure is the least informative thing on the box. These are more useful:

  • Response time, because a slow probe makes you pull it out and reinsert it, and reinsertion cools the food.
  • Resolution or step size, which is often a tenth of a degree and tells you the display is not faking precision.
  • The waterproof rating, because a probe that cannot be rinsed is a probe that gets rinsed anyway.
  • Whether the accuracy figure applies across the whole scale or only at one point.
  • Whether the device has been certified by an independent body, and by which one.

And the thing that beats accuracy

A thermometer does not know where the thickest part of your cut is. You do.

Push the probe into the thickest part, away from bone, away from the pan, deep enough that the tip is in the meat rather than near the edge. Leave it in while you cook. Read it without pulling it out, if the device allows it.

A well-placed probe in a cheap thermometer will beat a beautifully-made probe pushed into the fat at the edge of the rib. That is not a claim about these two products. It is just what the instrument is for.

Questions people ask

Does ±0.5°F mean it will read exactly right?

No. It is a bound on error under test conditions. The reading you get in your kitchen also contains placement error, ambient error and the cooking error you introduce yourself.

Is a thermometer with NIST certification worth more?

Certification means the instrument was tested against a national standard by an accredited laboratory, which is a meaningfully better claim than a manufacturer's own wording. We would take it as a tiebreaker rather than the main reason to buy.

What resolution should I look for?

Anything at or below 0.1°F is more resolution than most home cooks can use. Resolution is not accuracy, and a device can display a tenth of a degree while being less accurate than one that displays one degree.
