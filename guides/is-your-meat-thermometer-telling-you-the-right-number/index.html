Cooking thermometers · Guide

Is your meat thermometer telling you the right number?

A thermometer is only as useful as the number you compare it with.

By Affil3 editorial team · Published November 20, 2026 · Last reviewed November 20, 2026

The temperatures below are the US Department of Agriculture's published minimum internal temperatures, read from the FSIS chart on 2026-11-20. This guide does not test the accuracy of any thermometer.

The minimum internal temperatures

FoodMinimum internal temperatureRest time
Beef, pork, veal and lamb steaks, chops and roasts145 °F (62.8 °C)At least 3 minutes

Leftovers and casseroles165 °F (73.9 °C)—

The USDA adds that these are minimums and that a cook may choose to go higher for personal preference.

Where to put the probe

For a whole bird, the USDA says to check the innermost part of the thigh, the innermost part of the wing and the thickest part of the breast, avoiding bone. For poultry parts, insert the probe into the thickest area, avoiding bone. Bone heats faster than meat and will give you a reading that is too high.

Four questions to ask about any thermometer

  1. What range does the manufacturer state, and is it internal or ambient? The CHEF iQ Sense publishes both, and they are very different numbers: 32–212 °F internal against 32–1,000 °F ambient. The higher figure is not a reading range.
  2. How was accuracy established, and is there a certificate? A retail page saying a product is certified is not a certificate. Ask for the accredited laboratory and the certificate number.
  3. Can you calibrate it? The CHEF iQ Sense describes self-calibration with each measurement. An instant-read is usually checked against an ice bath or boiling water; the manual will say how.
  4. What does the app collect? A wireless probe often needs an account and a phone. Read the privacy policy before you connect it to your home network.

What this site can and cannot tell you

The two tracked thermometers have manufacturer-published specifications on their product pages, cited by source id. Neither has been tested by anyone here, and neither page repeats a certification as fact: the ThermoMaven page quotes ±0.5 °F as the maker's claim and notes that NIST's own FAQ says NIST does not endorse commercial equipment.
