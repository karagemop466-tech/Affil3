How to read an Amazon listing before you buy a kitchen appliance

The title is written to sell. The spec block is written to describe. You can tell which is which, and it takes about five minutes.

By ShopNow Kitchen editorial team · Published November 20, 2026 · Last reviewed November 20, 2026

Marketplace listings are written by sellers and by software. The maker's own product page is written by the maker. When the two disagree, the maker's page is usually right about the machine and the listing is usually right about the price and availability. Here is the method we use on our own shelf, written out so you can run it on anything.

Step one: open the maker's page first, not the listing

Search the product name on the maker's own site and open that page. Do not read the listing first. The listing will tell you which machine to look for; the maker's page will tell you what the machine is. Read in the other order and the listing's framing stays in your head, and the maker's page starts to read like a correction.

Step two: hunt the model number, not the marketing name

Every appliance has a model number somewhere in its details, and it is the only field that identifies a machine reliably. Our 8 qt air fryer is a live example of why. The listing's own copy names model RJ38-SQSS-8T, and Chefman publishes that model, stating a 200–400 °F range, 1700W/120V, dimensions of 15.4 by 12.6 by 13.5 in and a weight of 12.8 lb (S22). Chefman also publishes a different 8 qt machine, RJ38-8T-BLACK, with a 450 °F Hi-Fry finish and no stated dimensions (S12). Same capacity, two machines, one shelf. We publish only what the two pages agree on, and we publish the disagreement as well (see our Flagged for review page).

Step three: treat the title as advertising and the spec block as evidence

The title of that same listing advertises a 450 °F Hi-Fry option. The maker's page for the model the listing itself names states a 200–400 °F range, and 450 °F belongs to the maker's other 8 qt machine. A headline can be a promise about a product family; a specification block is a claim about one unit. Where they disagree, the specification block wins and the headline gets flagged (see our Flagged for review page).

Step four: check the four numbers that change the purchase

What to checkWhat we found on our own shelf
WattageThe listing states 1000 W. The maker's page for the same model number states 1700 W. We publish neither.
DimensionsThe listing states 12.75 by 13.5 by 10 in. The maker's page states 15.4 by 12.6 by 13.5 in. Two machines' footprints, published as one.
Temperature rangeThe listing's title says 450 °F. The maker's page for the model the listing names says 200–400 °F.
Certification claimsThe tracked thermometer's listing states NSF certification and an IP67 rating. The manufacturer's page states a plus or minus 0.5 °F accuracy and a waterproof design, and does not state an IP rating (see our Flagged for review page).

Step five: read what the maker does not say

Silence is data. When the maker's page gives a capacity but no dimensions, you can be certain the dimensions in the listing came from somewhere else. When a maker's page does not state a temperature range, the range on the listing is a claim rather than a specification. Our rule is that a blank cell stays blank: we would rather show a row of 'not stated' than promote a number we cannot stand behind.

The five-minute check, in order

  1. Open the maker's page first and find the model number.
  2. Open the listing and find the model number in its details.
  3. Compare wattage, size, temperature range and any certification claim. A disagreement is a question, not a rounding error.
  4. Check whether the maker's page is newer than the listing. Appliances get revised, and listing copy often does not.
  5. If the two pages disagree about something you care about, stop and ask the maker. One email to a manufacturer's contact form is faster than returning an appliance.

Questions readers ask next

Can the listing be wrong about the product?

Yes, and usually it is not wrong so much as describing a different variant of the same family. That is why the model number, not the product name, is the field to check.

Is a page on the maker's site always more reliable?

It is more reliable about the machine. It is not more reliable about price or availability, and it does not replace the maker's own warranty terms.

What if the maker has no page for it?

Then the listing's specifications are the only source, and they are unverified. We label those rows that way rather than treating a seller's copy as a specification.

Every fact on this site points at a dated, linked source. Where two official pages disagree, the disagreement is published rather than smoothed over.
