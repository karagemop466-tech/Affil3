Holiday cooking: what one countertop oven holds

Before you plan a holiday around a countertop oven, get four numbers straight: who it says it feeds, what it measures outside, what safety requires, and which thermometer you will use.

By ShopNow Kitchen editorial team · Published November 20, 2026 · Last reviewed November 20, 2026 · 780 words, about a 3 minute read

The short answer

The 26-quart Emeril French Door oven we track states its capacity as food for 4-6 people, in 24.6 litres, inside an outside box of 16.9 x 15.8 x 14.8 inches on 120 volts. No page we read states the largest bird that fits inside, so this guide does not estimate one. What it sets out instead: the USDA minimum temperatures that apply whatever heats the food (all poultry 165 °F), checking a large irregular roast in several places, and which of the two probes we track suits the day itself.

Every November the same advice circulates: put the bird in the air fryer and free up your oven. It can work. It also has three failure points that have nothing to do with technique - fit, food safety and timing - and two of the three can be settled from a specification sheet before you buy anything.

1. Fit: read the maker's serving number, not the quart number

Quarts are hard to picture, so use the manufacturer's own translation. The page for the 26 QT French Door oven we track states “26-Quart Capacity” with the line “preparing food for 4-6 people”, 24.6 litres, and outside dimensions of 16.9 in deep by 15.8 in wide by 14.8 in high. That is a household-size claim from the maker rather than a guess by us, and it is the honest limit of what can be said: none of the official pages we read states the largest bird that fits inside. If a listing tells you a specific weight of poultry fits, ask which page says so.

Then measure the counter, twice. The outside box is what competes with the coffee maker, and clearance is what decides whether the doors can open. A french-door oven is deeper than a basket fryer by design - that is the price of having rack positions.

2. Safety: target temperatures do not change with the appliance

A countertop oven does not get its own food-safety rules. From the USDA chart, the numbers that matter on a holiday: all poultry - breasts, whole bird, legs, thighs, wings, ground poultry, giblets and stuffing - at 165 °F; ground meats 160 °F; beef, pork, veal and lamb steaks, chops and roasts at 145 °F with at least a three-minute rest; leftovers and casseroles reheated to 165 °F. And the measurement is the part people skip: those temperatures are stated “as measured with a food thermometer”.

  • Do not judge by colour. USDA's factsheet is blunt that colour and grill marks are not indicators that food is safe, and cites its own study in which 21% of participants relied on visual cues for hamburger patties.
  • Check a large or irregularly shaped roast in several places, and check each piece separately - size, quantity and how food is stacked mean pieces reach temperature at different times (FSIS).
  • Put the probe in the thickest part, away from bone, fat and gristle (FSIS).
  • Keep perishable food out of the 40-140 °F range for more than two hours (FSIS). On a buffet day that is a serving decision, not a cooking one.

3. Timing: which thermometer earns a place at the table

Two shapes of thermometer, two different jobs on the day. A folding instant-read probe - the ThermoMaven row we track states a one-second read on a thermocouple sensor, a 100% waterproof body and a built-in magnet - is a spot check: you walk to the food. A leave-in wireless probe - the CHEF iQ Sense row is described by its maker with four internal sensors plus one ambient sensor and a Wi-Fi hub - watches the food while you are in another room. One person can carry the first through an entire meal. The second earns its keep when something is cooking for hours, or when the appliance sits in a room you keep leaving.

4. The plan, in the order that actually works

  1. Write the menu, then write the appliances: what runs in the countertop oven, what runs in the main oven, what only needs reheating.
  2. Give the countertop oven the shortest, least-attended job first. Sides and reheating are where a second chamber pays; the main roast is where an unexpected door opening costs the most.
  3. Decide where the thermometer sits before you start. An ambient probe in a small chamber that gets opened every few minutes will chase its own tail.
  4. Leave one rack position empty. A chamber that is 26 quarts with nothing in it is smaller with three pans and foil in it, and no page states a loaded capacity.
  5. Check temperature in more than one place, rest the meat for the time the chart asks for, and write the numbers down if you are cooking for people you are responsible for.

This is a planning guide, not a results guide. Nobody on this site cooked a holiday dinner in these machines, so there are no cook times, no crispiness claims and no brining advice. What is here comes from a manufacturer page, the observed listing and USDA FSIS guidance, each listed below.

Questions readers ask next

Can I cook a whole turkey in a 26-quart countertop oven?

No page we hold answers that, so neither do we. What we can tell you is what the maker states for that model - food for 4-6 people in 26 quarts - and that the fit question should be answered from a document that names a size or a weight, not from a volume figure.

Does the air fry function change the safe temperature?

No. Minimum internal temperatures are the same whatever heats the food, which is why this guide spends its space on measurement rather than on method.

Do I need a wireless probe for a holiday?

You need a thermometer. The wireless one buys you the option to leave the room and to watch the ambient temperature as well; an instant-read probe used on every piece does the safety job for a fraction of the price and with no app to open.
