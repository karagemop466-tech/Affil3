Questions to ask before buying a multi-function kitchen appliance

Multi-function appliances promise a dozen tools in one box. Ask which three you will actually use.

By ShopNow Kitchen editorial team · Published November 20, 2026 · Last reviewed November 20, 2026

Every appliance category eventually grows a multi-function flagship, and kitchen gear is no exception. The pitch is seductive: air fryer, toaster, oven, dehydrator, rotisserie, broiler, one plug. The reality, for most households, is that three functions do ninety-five percent of the work. Your job is to find out which three, and whether this box does them well.

Count the presets, then discount them

Presets are recipes, not features. One appliance we track advertises twenty-four one-touch preset options in its title; the number reads impressively, but think about what it means. You do not need twenty-four buttons to cook twenty-four foods. You need a temperature, a time, and the judgment to check early. Presets earn their keep for foods you cook on repeat; the rest are shortcuts you will never press. When you compare units, ignore the count and hunt for the presets matching your weekly foods.

A check we now run on every tracked appliance: the preset and accessory counts on the tracked 26-quart unit differ across official pages (flag FLG-024). Count from the listing's own spec table, and treat round numbers in marketing copy as marketing.

The accessory audit

Functions are delivered by accessories, and accessories are delivered by you to the sink. A rotisserie function implies a spit, forks, and a removal tool. Dehydrating implies racks, and hours of low heat that occupy the machine. Baking implies a pan or tray. One observed listing on our shelf titles six cooking accessories as included, which sounds generous until you imagine all six in the dishwasher. Ask for each advertised function: what physical part does it need, where does that part store, and who washes it.

Five questions before you buy

  1. Which three functions will I use in a normal week? Name the meals, not the modes.
  2. Does each of those functions have a dedicated, well-reviewed control path, or is it buried in a combo mode with compromises?
  3. What accessories does each function require, and are they included or extra purchases?
  4. How big is the learning curve, and does the manual (available as a PDF on most listings) read like a cookbook or a contract?
  5. Where do the accessories live between uses? Storage is the silent dealbreaker for combo appliances.

When a multi-function box is the right call

Combination appliances shine in three situations: small kitchens where three separate machines cannot fit; households that genuinely use the spread, like one person toasting at breakfast and air-frying at dinner; and renters or temporary setups where one box beats a move of many. If that is not your life, the honest math favors two good single-purpose tools over one crowd-pleasing box, even when the box looks like the better deal on paper.

One more angle from our shelf: the multi-function units we track sit in the same observed category (air fryers) as the basket models, but they are judged by buyers differently, on toast quality and oven evenness as much as frying. If you buy a combo unit, expect to judge it as an oven first; that is where most daily use lands.

Questions readers ask next

Do combo appliances cook worse than dedicated units?

They can, at the edges: a combo oven's air fry mode shares a cavity designed for baking, so crisping a single layer may take more attention than a dedicated basket. For most daily cooking the difference is manageable; for fries-every-night households it is the trade to test.

Is more presets better?

More presets is more buttons. The useful count is how many presets match foods you already cook weekly.

What is the first function to skip?

For most buyers: dehydrating or rotisserie. Both are occasional-use modes that carry permanent accessory storage and washing costs.
