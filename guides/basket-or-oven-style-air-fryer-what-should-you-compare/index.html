Basket or oven-style air fryer: what should you compare?

The shape decides more than the brand does. Start with the food you cook most, then compare the three numbers that actually differ.

By Affil3 editorial team· Published November 20, 2026· Specifications checked November 20, 2026· 6 sections

Two air fryers can hold the same number of quarts and still cook very differently, because a basket and a countertop oven are not the same machine with a different shell. One has a drawer you pull out and shake. The other has a door, racks, and heat coming from more than one direction. Most buying advice skips this and starts with brands.

What actually changes between the two shapes

A basket fryer moves hot air over food sitting in a single perforated drawer. You pull the drawer, shake, and push it back. An oven-style unit puts food on a rack or tray inside a box with a door, so you can use more than one level and you can put a pan in it. That single difference drives everything else: how much fits, how evenly it browns, how much counter it eats, and how much of it you can put in a dishwasher.

Question one: are you cooking two things at once?

If dinner is usually a main and a side that need different temperatures, a dual basket is the smallest thing that solves it. The CHEFMAN 6 Qt gives you two independent 3-quart drawers, and its Sync Finish setting runs both timers so they land together (S11). That is a real convenience, and it is the reason this shape exists.

The catch is arithmetic. Two 3-quart baskets are each half the size of one 6-quart drawer, so a single big batch of fries gets split across two drawers and you still end up shaking both. If your cooking is mostly one big pile of one thing, the extra basket buys you nothing.

Question two: how much of the counter can you give up?

This is where the oven shape loses. The Emeril Lagasse 26 Qt measures 16.9 inches deep, 15.8 wide and 14.8 tall by the manufacturer's own figures (S13). That is not a small appliance, and it needs clearance for a door that swings toward you. A basket unit at 13.38 × 11.81 × 10.63 inches (S11) sits in a corner and gets pushed back when you are done.

If you have a small kitchen, measure the depth of your counter before anything else. A deep oven-style unit that has to live on the dining table will annoy you every day, and no amount of capacity makes up for that.

Question three: do you want to see the food, or open the drawer?

The dual-basket Chefman has a window on each basket and an interior light (S11), so checking on food does not dump the heat. The 8 Qt Chefman does not list a window at all; checking means pulling the basket, which is exactly when the heat leaves. This sounds like a small thing until it is the fourth time you have opened it.

Windows are not a substitute for a rack, though. If you want to bake a 12-inch pizza or run a rotisserie, an oven-shaped box with a door is the only one of the three that can do it.

What none of these pages will tell you

None of the three manufacturer pages states how evenly the unit browns food, how loud the fan is, or how long it takes to come up to temperature. Those are the things a review is supposed to measure, and this site has not tested any of these units. What you are reading is a specification comparison, not a test.

Three more things are genuinely unresolved. The Emeril page gives two different preset counts in the same page. The Chefman 8 Qt is the worst of them: the Amazon listing and Chefman's own product page describe different machines, with different model numbers, wattages, temperature ranges and finishes, which is why that product's page withholds four specifications. All of it is recorded on the Flagged for review page by number rather than smoothed over.

A short version

  1. Two foods, two timings, small kitchen: dual basket, and accept the smaller drawers.
  2. One big pile of one thing, family portions: single large basket, and check whether the model you are buying is the one the listing describes.
  3. Whole meals, baking, rotisserie, and you have the counter depth: oven-style, measured before you buy.
  4. No counter depth and no patience for batches: none of these. A sheet pan and a normal oven is cheaper.
