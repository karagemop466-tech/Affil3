## The two machines are not competing, and that is the whole problem

You are looking at two listings. Both have "air fryer" in the name. One is a basket with a lid; the other is a box with a glass door and two shelves inside. Whichever you buy, you will cook roughly the same foods. The decision is not about cooking. It is about your counter, your sink and the way you actually cook dinner on a Wednesday.

## Four lines to write down first

Do this before you read another product page. It takes three minutes and it settles most of the argument.

1. **The strip.** Measure the width and depth of the counter space the machine may occupy. Add nothing. A listing's dimensions are the box, and the box includes handles.
2. **The ceiling.** Measure from the counter to the underside of the cabinet above it. Basket machines are tall in a way that photos hide, and an oven-style unit needs air above it, not a wood shelf.
3. **The swing.** A drawer-style basket needs the depth of the drawer pulled out. A french door needs the width of the door open. Picture it with the machine loaded and a person standing in front of it.
4. **The sink.** Put the largest basket or tray you intend to wash into your sink. If it does not lie flat, it will be rinsed at an angle over a splash guard for the rest of its life, which is a real cost that no spec sheet carries.

## Where the numbers flatter the machine

**Quarts are volume, not capacity.** A basket cooks crisp results in a single layer. A six-quart basket holding six quarts of stacked wings cooks steamed wings in the middle. The usable number for crisp food is the floor area of one layer, which listings rarely give you and which you can estimate from the photo of the basket with a ruler in frame.

**Two baskets are not one bigger basket.** Our twin-basket row is titled as two independent three-quart baskets. That answers a question about *two components* — potatoes and chicken, say — and does nothing at all for a whole bird or a rack of ribs, because neither half is large enough. If your real problem is one large item, the eight-quart single basket and the 26-quart oven are the only candidates in this set.

**Preset counts are about the control panel.** "24 one-touch preset options" is a number of buttons. Ask what you will use twice a week — usually air fry, roast, and reheat — and whether you can set time and temperature independently of a preset. If you cannot, the presets are the interface, and you will live inside whichever two you like.

**"Dishwasher safe" is a claim about a material, not about your rack.** Nonstick coatings and dishwasher heat have a long, boring history together. Check the basket's height against your top rack before you plan on it.

## What a basket does better, and what it does worse

A basket is fast because it is small: the air path is short and the cavity fills with food, so preheat is quick and the machine is often done before an oven would be preheated. It is also easy to forget about in a good way — you can shake a basket mid-cook without opening a door and losing your heat.

Its weaknesses are physical, not technical. A tall item does not fit. Four servings means either a crowded single layer or two rounds, and two rounds means the first one is cooling while the second finishes. Cleaning is one part, but that part is greasy and coated.

An oven-style unit is the opposite trade. You get racks, so you get vertical capacity and the ability to cook something that has height. You get a door you can look through, which matters more than people expect when food is browning unevenly. You also get more surface to wipe, a bigger box that needs a permanent home, and a cavity that takes longer to come up to temperature because it is holding more air.
