First week

Six things to cook first in a new air fryer

The first week decides whether an appliance becomes a habit or a cupboard resident. These are the six cooks that make the case for leaving it on the counter.

SN By ShopNow Labs editorial desk · Published 2026-11-20 · 7 min read
I get commissions for purchases made through links in this post.

Why the first week matters

An air fryer that sits in a cupboard is an expensive mistake. The appliance that earns its counter space is the one that gets used on a night when you cannot be bothered to cook. So the first week should not be ambitious. It should be six things that are faster in the fryer than in the oven, so that the habit forms before the novelty wears off.

These are technique notes, not recipes. Quantities and times vary by appliance and by how full the basket is, and we are not going to print a time that pretends otherwise.

The six

1. Frozen fries, and the honest verdict

This is the test every air fryer is judged by, and the one where the difference between models is most obvious.

The technique that matters: do not fill the basket. A single layer, or close to it, gets you crisp fries. A packed basket steams them. If your household wants a large portion, this is exactly where a single 8-quart basket beats two 3-quart drawers, because a bigger single layer means a bigger batch.

Shake the basket partway through. Every time. The ones at the edge brown first.

2. Chicken wings

Wings are where an air fryer genuinely outperforms an oven, because the moving air crisps the skin while the inside stays moist. Pat them dry before they go in; surface moisture is the enemy of crisp.

This is also the cook that makes the strongest case for a probe. Wings are uneven, and the thickest one is not the one on top. If you have a thermometer, use it on the thickest piece rather than trusting the timer.

3. Roasted vegetables, in a single layer

Broccoli, cauliflower, peppers, Brussels sprouts. Toss in a little oil, season, single layer, shake once.

The failure mode is crowding. Vegetables release water; in a crowded basket that water becomes steam and you get soft vegetables with brown edges instead of roasted ones. This is a capacity lesson disguised as a recipe: the usable capacity of any air fryer is smaller than its stated capacity, because you cannot fill it and still get a roast.

4. Reheating, which is the underrated use

Pizza, fried chicken, pastries, leftovers that go soggy in a microwave. A few minutes of moving hot air restores texture that a microwave destroys.

This is the use case that justifies counter space, because it happens several times a week and takes less time than preheating a full oven. An oven-style unit like the Emeril is especially good at this because of the flat rack space; a basket is better for a single portion.

5. A whole chicken or a large cut

This is where the shape question becomes concrete.

  • Oven style: the cavity is designed for a whole bird. The 26-quart Emeril is the shape for this job.
  • Single large basket: an 8-quart drawer can take a small whole chicken, but check the height clearance before you count on it.
  • Dual basket: a 3-quart drawer will not take a whole chicken. Two drawers of portions is the correct use.

If whole-bird cooking is your goal, you have already answered the basket-versus-oven question.

6. Something you would normally deep fry

Small batches of anything breaded: nuggets, fish fingers, arancini, tofu. The technique that separates good from bad is a light oil spray on the surface, not a pool of oil in the basket.

Accept that breaded items will drop crumbs. Line the drawer with a paper liner if your model allows it — the Chefman 8-quart listing's review summary mentions liners specifically — and accept that you will still clean the drawer.

Set the expectation low on day one

Almost nobody's first air fryer cook is their best. The first two or three cooks calibrate you to the appliance: how full is too full, how much shaking a batch needs, how far the timer is from the reality. Plan for that, and the fourth cook will be genuinely good.

What not to cook first

  • A huge batch of anything. It will disappoint, and the disappointment will be about technique, not the appliance. Start small.
  • Anything with a wet batter. Wet batter runs through the basket before it sets. Breaded and dry-coated works; poured batter does not.
  • A whole chicken in a dual-basket drawer. It does not fit, and forcing it blocks the airflow the whole design depends on.

The one accessory worth adding

A probe, if you cook proteins. It converts "is it done" from a guess into a number, and it works in the oven and on the grill as well as in the fryer. On this desk that is the CHEF iQ Sense, and its role is described in that guide.

Paper liners are the other cheap addition, for cleanup rather than cooking quality.

A first-week plan

| Night | Cook | What it teaches you | |---|---|---| | 1 | Frozen fries, single layer | How full is too full | | 2 | Wings, shaken once | How the basket browns unevenly | | 3 | Roasted vegetables | Why crowding steams instead of roasts | | 4 | Leftover pizza or fried chicken | The habit-forming use case | | 5 | Breaded nuggets | Where the crumbs go | | 6 | Something from your normal rotation | Whether it earns the counter |

If night six goes well, keep it out. If it does not, the honest problem is probably shape rather than model, and the capacity checklist is the next read.

A note on times and temperatures

This guide deliberately contains no cooking times or temperatures for specific foods, because they depend on the appliance, the load and the starting temperature, and because we have not tested these units. For food-safety targets, use authoritative guidance such as the USDA rather than a buying site. Appliance-specific times should come from the manual that ships with your unit.
