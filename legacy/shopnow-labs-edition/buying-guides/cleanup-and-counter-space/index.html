Buyer's notebook

How to assess cleanup and counter-space trade-offs

You will wash this thing more often than you will admire it. And it will occupy the same square of counter for years. Here is how to price both before you buy.

SN By ShopNow Labs editorial desk · Published 2026-11-20 · 7 min read
I get commissions for purchases made through links in this post.

Two costs, both recurring

Every appliance review talks about the first cook. Almost none talk about the four-hundredth. The two costs that show up four hundred times are washing up and floor-plan.

They are also the two costs a listing is least likely to tell you the truth about, because "dishwasher safe" and "compact design" are marketing phrases, not measurements.

Part one: cleanup

Count the grease-touching parts

This is the whole method. Take the listing's "what's in the box" list and count every object that food or grease will touch.

  • Single basket. One basket, usually one crisper plate or tray inside it. Two parts.
  • Dual basket. Two baskets, two trays. Four parts, and two of them are awkward shapes.
  • Oven style. One or more racks, a drip tray, and whatever accessories ship with it. The Emeril listing states six accessories included. That is potentially eight or more items to wash, plus the interior.

More parts is not automatically worse, because a big oven rack can go a week between washes while a basket needs washing every time. The question is not "how many" but "how many, how often, and do they fit where I wash things".

The sink test

Do the dimensions, not the description. A dishwasher-safe basket that is wider than your dishwasher's usable rack width is a hand-wash item. A rack that is 30 inches wide will not go in a standard sink at all.

Before ordering, find the basket or rack dimensions on the listing. If they are not stated, assume the largest dimension is closer to the appliance's exterior width than you would like, and plan accordingly.

Coating, and what happens to it

Both Chefman listings state nonstick, dishwasher-safe baskets. Nonstick that goes through a dishwasher repeatedly tends to degrade faster than nonstick that is hand-washed, regardless of what the label permits. Dishwasher-safe means it will survive the wash, not that the coating will look new in three years.

One review excerpt displayed on the Chefman 8-quart listing raises a related concern about the inner grill's coating wearing. That is a single customer comment, not a test result. Treat it as a prompt to inspect the coating when yours arrives, not as a verdict.

The cheap accessory that changes the math

Disposable paper liners, mentioned in a customer review on the Chefman 8-quart listing, cut basket washing dramatically. They are not included, they are a small extra purchase, and they do not eliminate cleaning because grease still reaches the element and the drawer. But if the honest reason you might not use the appliance is the washing-up, they are the cheapest fix available.

The interior

An oven-style unit accumulates grease on the inside walls, the door glass and the element guard. A basket unit's grease is mostly contained in the drawer. That is a genuine advantage for basket designs and a genuine ongoing chore for oven designs.

Part two: counter space

Measure three numbers, not one

Manufacturers publish exterior dimensions, but the number that matters is the space the appliance occupies and uses.

  1. Footprint. Width × depth of the base.
  2. Vertical clearance. Height, plus enough room to lift out a drawer or open a top door. Under a wall cabinet, this is where most purchases go wrong.
  3. Working clearance. In front, for a drawer to pull out fully or for doors to swing. A French-door oven needs the door arc; a drawer needs the full drawer depth.

Add those up and compare them with the actual counter you have, not the counter you wish you had. A 26-quart oven is a large object; the Emeril is a countertop oven with French doors and is described in Amazon's review summary as taking up significant counter space by at least one customer.

The appliance that replaces something is free

A countertop oven that replaces a toaster oven costs zero additional footprint. A second air fryer next to an existing one costs its full footprint forever.

Ask directly: what leaves the counter when this arrives? If the answer is "nothing", you are not buying an appliance, you are buying permanent square footage in the most contested part of the kitchen.

Where it will actually live

Storage is not a solution for a heavy appliance. If the plan is "I'll put it in the cupboard and take it out when I need it", be honest about the weight and the awkwardness. A large oven-style unit that lives in a cupboard will be used far less than a basket unit that lives on the counter.

Putting a number on it

A simple scoring method, done on paper before you order:

| Question | If yes | If no | |---|---|---| | Does it fit under my cabinet with the drawer or door open? | keep | stop | | Do the grease-touching parts fit my sink or dishwasher? | keep | expect hand-washing | | Does something leave the counter when it arrives? | keep | it costs full footprint | | Will I leave it on the counter, or store it? | keep | it will be used less | | Are there more than four parts to wash each time? | plan for it | keep | | Does it need a dedicated circuit or draw a lot of watts? | check your kitchen | keep |

Anything that fails a "stop" row is not a trade-off, it is a mismatch. Everything else is a preference.
