## The problem with the number

An air fryer's capacity is stated as a volume: 6 quarts, 8 quarts, 26 quarts. Volume tells you how much empty space is inside. It does not tell you the shape of that space, how many separate spaces there are, or how much of it you can actually load without blocking airflow.

Air frying depends on hot air moving around the food. A basket packed to the lid does not cook like a basket packed to two-thirds. So the usable capacity is always smaller than the stated capacity, and how much smaller depends on the shape.

## Four numbers that are not the same number

**Nominal capacity.** What the listing says. The Chefman dual basket is 6 quarts. The TurboFry is 8 quarts. The Emeril oven is 26 quarts.

**Divided capacity.** Whether that volume is one space or several. The 6-quart Chefman is two 3-quart drawers. You cannot put a 5-quart batch in a 6-quart dual basket; the biggest single batch is 3 quarts. This is the single most misread number in the category.

**Shaped capacity.** A tall narrow drawer and a wide shallow oven cavity can hold the same volume and fit completely different food. A whole chicken needs height. A pizza needs a flat diameter. Racks need width. Volume does not predict any of those.

**Usable capacity.** The volume you can fill while leaving air room. In practice this is the number that matters and the number nobody publishes.

:::note The one-line test
Ask: "what is the largest single item that fits in this, in the flat position?" If the listing cannot answer that, the quart number is not doing any work for you.
:::

## The checklist

Work down this list against any listing. If an item cannot be answered from the listing, that is information too: it means the manufacturer did not think it mattered, or did not want to say.

**1. Is the stated capacity one space or the sum of several?**
For a dual-zone unit, write down the per-zone figure, not the total. The Chefman dual basket is 3 quarts per side.

**2. What is the interior shape?**
Width, depth and height. A drawer is a box; an oven is a wider, flatter box. Look for interior dimensions in the product details or the images, not in the marketing paragraph.

**3. Does the food I cook most often fit flat?**
Pizza, a whole chicken, a sheet of vegetables, four portions of fries. Picture each one inside the dimensions. This is more accurate than any capacity number.

**4. How many zones, at what temperatures?**
Two zones let you run 400°F on one side and 350°F on the other. Racks in a single cavity do not. Count zones, not racks.

**5. Will a full load still cook evenly?**
More food means less air movement. A larger basket that you fill to the top will need shaking or turning. Factor in whether the design makes that easy — a drawer you pull out is easier to shake than a rack inside a hot oven.

**6. Can it fit where you plan to put it?**
Exterior width, depth and height, plus clearance above (to lift a drawer or open a door) and in front (to pull a drawer or swing a door). The Emeril's doors swing forward; a drawer needs pull-out room.

**7. Does the basket or rack fit your sink or dishwasher?**
This is capacity in the practical sense. A big basket that does not fit in your sink is a bigger cleanup job than a small one.

**8. How much power does it draw?**
Wattage is stated on most listings. A high-wattage appliance on a shared kitchen circuit is a real constraint in older homes. Check the listing's wattage against your kitchen before assuming you can run it alongside a kettle.

The honest read of that table: the listings give you capacity and little else. Which means the decision cannot be made from the listing alone. It has to be made from the shape of what you cook.

:::warn Do not trust a "serves X people" claim
Serving counts are marketing. A 3-quart drawer and an 8-quart basket will both claim to serve a family. One of them holds a single layer of fries. Convert servings into items you can picture, then check the interior dimensions.
:::

## The version that fits on a card

- Divide the capacity by the number of zones before comparing anything.
- Compare interior shape, not interior volume.
- The largest single item that fits flat is the number that decides most purchases.
- Measure your counter, including clearance, before you order.
- If the listing does not state interior dimensions, you are buying on the photos.

When you have narrowed to a shape, the next question is usually [cleanup and counter space](/buying-guides/cleanup-and-counter-space/), because that is what you live with daily rather than what you notice on day one.
