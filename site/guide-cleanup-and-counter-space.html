The two costs nobody puts on the box

How to assess cleanup and counter-space trade-offs

The appliance you keep is the one that is annoying to wash least. A concrete way to price cleanup time and footprint before you buy, using only what a listing page already tells you.

Counter Tested 2026-11-30 3 min read no hands-on testing on this page Topic: counter-space
Sources used for anything factual here: S06 S16 S27.

There is a pattern in how small appliances die: not broken, banished. The unit that made a good Friday dinner becomes the thing in a cupboard by April, because the cost of using it is paid four times a week in washing-up and once a day in counter real estate, and nobody totals those costs before buying.

You can total them in about fifteen minutes, from the listing page and a tape measure.

Pricing the cleanup

Write four numbers. They are all findable on a product page if you look for the accessory diagram instead of the bullet points.

  • Parts count. Basket, inner tray, outer drawer, rack, crumb tray, lid, window seal. Every one is a thing you will handle while the meal is going cold. Anything past four parts is a Sunday appliance for most households.
  • Dishwasher-safe, stated how? "Dishwasher safe basket" and "all parts except the main unit are dishwasher safe" are different claims. Look for the exact sentence and what it excludes. A coating that must be hand-washed "to preserve the non-stick finish" is a hand-washed coating, forever.
  • Coating or bare metal. Coated baskets wash fast and wear out. Bare stainless lasts decades and needs a soak. Pick which problem you would rather have; there is no third option.
  • Grease path. Does fat drip into a catch tray you can rinse, or onto a heating element area you are nervous about touching? The second design quietly becomes the appliance you avoid after a fatty cook.

Multiply parts count by about two minutes per part and you have a rough per-use cleanup cost in the currency that actually matters: the minutes between dinner and sitting down. If it is more than eight, be honest about how often you will pay it.

Pricing the counter

Counter space is the scarcest resource in a kitchen and it is measured in centimetres you already own, not in "compact" marketing.

  1. The rectangle it occupies, plus the clearance the manual demands around the vents. The published dimensions in the bullet points are usually the body only.
  2. The swing. A drawer unit needs the front clear and about again its own depth to open. An oven unit needs headroom; check the cabinet above before you check the price.
  3. The cable. Short cords force the appliance into an inconvenient spot, and an inconvenient spot is why appliances end up in cupboards. Where the outlet is decides where the appliance lives, and therefore who uses it.
  4. The hot landing. Somewhere heat-proof has to sit next to it for a basket that comes out at cooking temperature. If the only free counter is a wooden block, that is a real cost.
  5. The daily tax. If it cannot stay out, it must be fetched and returned — about 45 seconds each way, plus the drawer of accessories. That is the tax that kills the habit, and it is completely invisible in a spec table.

Note

note One decisive question

Ask: on a Wednesday, after work, is this faster than the oven? If the answer is no for the meals you actually cook, the appliance is a weekend project and you should buy it as one, at a price that reflects that.

The trade-off that decides everything

Big capacity and low friction are opposed, and the listings never say so. A 9-quart oven-style unit wins a dinner party and loses a Tuesday. A 4-quart basket wins Tuesday and gets you cooking in batches on the days you have people over.

Pick the day you are buying for. Most households buying their first one are buying for Tuesdays and imagining dinner parties; the cupboard is full of the result.

What we deliberately have not claimed

No durability figures, no coating-life claims, no decibel numbers, no "easiest to clean" verdict. Those need weeks of ownership and a consistent method, which we do not have yet, and a claim we cannot stand behind is not worth a page view — the FTC's guidance on endorsements exists precisely so that a recommendation cannot quietly become an advertisement for a guess, and Amazon's own rules bar inaccurate or overbroad product claims.

What we will do, if a unit arrives: same washes, same batches, same tape-measured dimensions, published with the misses in. That is the only version of "we tested it" worth printing here.
