Basket or oven-style air fryer: what should you compare?

Two boxes, two prices, nearly identical marketing copy. Here are the four things that actually change how you cook, and how to check each one on a listing page in about ten minutes.

Open two product pages for air fryers at once and read them side by side. You will struggle to tell them apart. Same adjectives, same "8-in-1" badge, same claim that it uses "75% less oil" — a comparison against deep frying, which almost nobody is deciding between at 11pm on a Tuesday.

The differences are real. They are just not in the marketing copy. They are in four places on the listing page, and once you know where to look you can compare two appliances faster than you can read a single "best air fryers 2026" roundup.

1. How much food fits in one layer

A basket and an oven-style unit can quote the same number of quarts and hold completely different amounts of dinner, because the number describes the cavity, not the cooking surface. Food cooks unevenly and steams instead of browning when it is stacked.

So ignore the headline capacity and find the cooking area. If the listing gives internal dimensions, multiply them and compare areas, not volumes. If it does not, look at the accessory image: a basket with a shallow, wide footprint holds more usable food than a deep narrow one with a bigger number on the box.

Note

note Work this out for your own household

Count the portions you cook most often, not the holiday you are imagining. Then ask the question the listing cannot answer: does one batch feed that number, or do you need two, with the second one sitting in a low oven going soft? Two-batch cooking is the honest cost of a small appliance, and it is a per-meal cost, not a one-off.

2. Whether you can see through the door

Oven-style units with a window and an interior light let you check colour without interrupting cooking. Basket units make you pull the drawer, which is fine for a shake-halfway food (fries, wings) and bad for anything that hates a temperature drop.

This is not a spec. It is a habit question: do you cook things you need to watch, or things you can walk away from?

3. What "multifunction" adds to your kitchen, and what it takes

Dehydrate, roast, bake, proof, air-fry — the same heating element and fan doing different labelled jobs. Some of those jobs genuinely replace a gadget you already own; most replace a gadget you have never bought.

Before you pay for eight functions, name the two you will use weekly. If the extra functions belong to a device you already own and like, you are buying a longer menu you will not read. If you own nothing that roasts on a weeknight, the same box may be the only oven you use until January.

4. The counter it has to live on

The appliance does not need 0.6 square feet. It needs 0.6 square feet plus clearance on the vented sides, plus a path to an outlet, plus somewhere to put the hot basket mid-cook, plus the height to open a drawer without hitting the cabinet above. Measure the space first, then shop. Counter space is the scarcest thing in most kitchens, and no review can measure yours.

A worked example you can do at home

Take the wattage from the listing and divide it by your mains voltage. On a nominal 120-volt circuit, a 1,500-watt appliance draws 12.5 amps (1500 ÷ 120). A 15 or 20-amp kitchen circuit that already carries a microwave and a kettle will not thank you for adding a third big load, and a nuisance-tripping breaker is a bad first week with a new gadget. Do the arithmetic with your own numbers: the panel tells you the breaker rating, the listing tells you the watts, and the division is the whole method.

What we have not done

We have not cooked in either style this month. Nobody at Counter Tested has a unit to test yet, and we are not going to borrow a lab result from someone else's article and present it as ours. What we have done is read the programme rules and the FTC's endorsement guidance closely enough to know that "we compared 12 models" is a claim you have to be able to stand behind.

So this page is a method, not a verdict. It should still be useful, because the four questions above decide most real purchases, and none of them require a testing kitchen to ask.
