Buying for the appliance you already own

Questions to ask before buying a multi-function kitchen appliance

Eight functions, one element. The six questions that separate an appliance that replaces three gadgets from one that becomes a fourth.

Counter Tested 2026-11-30 3 min read no hands-on testing on this page Topic: air-fryers
Sources used for anything factual here: S06 S15 S16 S27.

A multi-function cooker is not eight appliances. It is one heating element, one fan and one control board with eight names on the buttons. Sometimes that is a bargain. Often it is a microwave with ambition.

Six questions, in the order that saves the most money.

1. Which two functions will you use in a normal week?

Say them out loud, and say the day. "Air fry" and "roast" on a Wednesday are a real use case. "Dehydrate" and "proof" are two functions most of us buy for a phase of life rather than a week.

The arithmetic that matters is simple: if the two functions you will use weekly are already covered by an appliance you own and like, you are paying for a longer menu. If they are not — if the thing you own is a decade-old oven that takes twenty minutes to come up to temperature — a multi-function box may genuinely retire a task.

2. What does it replace, and can you sell that thing?

A real test, and an honest one: if you would not part with the appliance it claims to replace, the replacement has not happened, and you now own two machines fighting for the same counter. Count what you would actually list on a marketplace, not what you would keep "just in case".

3. Which functions are marketing names for the same mode?

"Crisp", "air fry", "roast" and "bake" on many units are the same convection cycle with a different default temperature and time. "Dehydrate" is that cycle held at a low temperature for many hours, which requires a fan that can run that long without you wanting to. Check whether the function list has distinct temperature ranges published — if it does, the functions are probably real. If the listing only gives names, you are reading a menu, not a specification.

4. What is the control actually like at 7pm?

Two things ruin an appliance faster than performance: a dial with no numbers, and a touchscreen with a five-step menu for a common task. You want the mode you use most to be reachable in one action.

You cannot see this in a bullet list. You can: search the listing's image set for a close-up of the panel, and read the questions section for the phrase "how do I" — repeated "how do I do the simple thing" questions tell you more than any review score.

5. Does the cavity fit the food you cook, in the shape you cook it?

A fan at the back means the front half of a tray cooks slower. A round cavity wastes the corners of a square pan. Height under the element decides whether a loaf rises into heat. If you cook tray meals, geometry beats capacity, and a smaller unit with a wide flat floor will beat a bigger one that fits nothing you own.

6. Who cleans it after the third use, not the first?

Multi-function units have more places for grease to go: a window seal, a mesh behind the element, a crumb tray under a tray, a lid with a gasket. Every one of those is a job you inherit for the life of the appliance, and cleaning a gasket is not in any hero image.

Note

note A rule of thumb worth more than a review

If you can name the appliance it retires, buy the functions. If you cannot, buy the simplest box that does the one job you actually cook, and put the difference into a pan.

What we are not going to tell you

That any specific model is "the best value", that a brand is reliable, or that an eight-function unit will earn its counter. Those are conclusions about durability and about other people's kitchens, and we do not have the ownership history to make them.
