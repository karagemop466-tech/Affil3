Black Friday kitchen deals: how to tell a real discount from a fake one

Deal week pricing is an observation, not a promise. A short checklist for judging kitchen discounts while the banners are blinking.

It's deal week, and every appliance on every site is wearing a percentage-off badge. Some of those discounts are real. Some are arithmetic on a number nobody ever paid. Here's how we think about it — including on this site, where every price we show is stamped with the day we observed it.

What is a "list price," really?

Amazon states that the List Price shown on a listing is provided by the manufacturer, supplier or seller, and that displaying one depends on purchase and offer history over roughly the past 90 days — with the explicit caveat that list prices "may not necessarily reflect the product's prevailing market price" (Amazon's List Price help page). In other words: the crossed-out number is context, not history.

The six checks

  1. Compare against the last 30–90 days, not the crossed-out number. Price-history tools exist, but even a plain memory check ("what did this cost in October?") defeats most fake urgency.
  2. Read which offer the price belongs to. Listings can show several sellers and conditions. A used-like-new price sitting next to a new price is not a discount on the new one — we observed exactly that combination on a listing in our register this week.
  3. Check the return window before the deal, not after. Holiday return policies (many items bought in November–December 2026 can be returned through January 31, 2027) make impulse buys less risky — but confirm the item qualifies.
  4. Price the parts too. A cheap unit with $35 replacement baskets isn't cheap. The manual or listing usually says what's available separately.
  5. Watch the "per count" trick. Multi-packs and bundles change the unit math. The per-unit figure is printed right on the listing if you look.
  6. Set your number first. Decide what the thing is worth to you before the countdown timer starts. The deal is only good relative to the price you'd actually pay.

How this site handles deal prices

When we show a price — like the deal prices observed on our register products on November 20, 2026 — it's a photograph, not a promise: what Amazon displayed at that moment, with the listing linked as the source of truth. We don't run "lowest price ever" claims because we can't substantiate them, and we don't compute savings forecasts. If a price matters to your decision, click through and look at the current one. That's not modesty; it's the only honest way to publish prices that change hourly.
