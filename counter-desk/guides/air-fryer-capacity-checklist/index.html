A no-hype checklist for comparing air fryer capacity

Quarts are the easiest number on the box to misread. Six checks, in the order that matters.

By The Counter Desk editorial desk· Published November 20, 2026· Specifications checked November 20, 2026· 6 minute read

The short version

  • Check whether a capacity figure is for the whole unit or for one basket.
  • Litres and quarts are printed inconsistently; divide one by the other rather than assuming.
  • The usable volume is smaller than the stated volume, always.
  • Feeds-N people is a manufacturer's estimate, not a measurement.

Capacity is the first number on an air fryer box and the one that gets compared most. It is also the one stated most loosely. Here is the order to read it in.

1. Total, or per basket?

Chefman's 6-quart dual-basket unit is 6 quarts because it has two 3-quart baskets, and the manufacturer says so plainly on its own page S10. That is a different cooking proposition from a single 6-quart drawer: same number, half the usable depth in each chamber. When a listing says '6 Quart' and a spec row says '2 × 3Qt', both are true and only one is useful.

2. Do the metric conversion yourself

Manufacturers often print litres beside quarts, and the two do not always round the same way. Chefman's 6-quart unit is listed as 5.67 L S10; its 8-quart unit is listed as 7.57 L S14. One US quart is about 0.946 litres, so those figures are consistent — but only because you checked. Treat a pair of numbers that does not convert as a sign that one of them was copied from a different model.

3. Subtract the hardware

Stated capacity is the chamber, not the space left after the crisper plate, the rack, the rotisserie spit or the heating element guard. A 26-quart oven with a rotisserie installed has less room for a tray than the number suggests. No manufacturer in this set publishes a usable-after-accessories figure, so the honest answer is: it is smaller than the box says, and you will not know by how much until it arrives.

4. Convert quarts into the food you actually cook

A more useful unit than the quart is the handful. Two 3-quart baskets hold roughly two generous portions of fries each, or one portion plus a vegetable. A single 8-quart basket holds a family portion of wings in one layer. These are estimates from volume, not measurements, and they are the sort of estimate a shop should label as an estimate.

What capacity does *not* tell you is speed. A larger basket does not cook faster; it usually cooks slower per batch because there is more cold food in the chamber at once. Anyone claiming otherwise is selling a number rather than a machine.

5. Read the 'feeds' figure as a manufacturer's estimate

Emeril states its 26-quart oven prepares food for 4–6 people S12; Chefman states its 8-quart unit feeds four to six S14. Those are the makers' own characterisations, printed without a serving size attached. They are useful as a rough ordering and useless as a specification. If you cook for four with leftovers planned, assume you are at the top of any stated range.

6. Write down what the page did not say

Interior dimensions are the number you actually need and the number manufacturers publish least often. All three manufacturer pages read here publish exterior dimensions — Chefman for both the 6-quart and the 8-quart unit S10 S14, Emeril for its 26-quart oven S12 — and none of them publishes the inside of the chamber. When the interior is missing, the follow-up is the manual or an email to the maker, not a guess from a photo.

Worth knowing

A checker you can use before you buy: measure the largest item you cook regularly (a whole chicken, a sheet-pan dinner, a 12-inch pizza) and compare it to the exterior footprint minus two inches on each side. It is crude, and crude beats optimistic.
